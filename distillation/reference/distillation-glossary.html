Reference · Distilling the summarizer

Distillation glossary The vocabulary this course uses — and the two or three words that decide which method you are allowed to use.

Every lesson in this workspace uses these terms with these meanings. Where a term is ambiguous in the wider literature, the ambiguity is called out rather than smoothed over, because most of it is the difference between two incompatible recipes.

The one-line distinction

Soft targets copy the teacher's probability distribution and need a shared output vocabulary. Hard targets copy the teacher's generated text and need nothing shared at all. Your teacher and student have vocabularies of 151,669 and 248,320, so only the second is available to you.

01The cast

Knowledge distillation the whole idea

Training a small model (the student) to reproduce the behaviour of a large one (the teacher), rather than training it on human-written labels alone. The premise is that a strong model's outputs encode things the labels do not — how it hedges, what it considers important, which details it drops.

Origin: Hinton, Vinyals & Dean, 2015
Teacher this project

Your fine-tuned Qwen3-8B + LoRA in finetune/qwen3-8b-lora/. It is the model whose house style and judgement you are trying to preserve.

Student this project

Qwen3.5-2B — 2B parameters, a hybrid Gated DeltaNet / Gated Attention architecture, vocab 248,320. The model that has to run on your laptop's CPU.

Its tokenizer is what closes the soft-target route — see vocabulary mismatch.

02What crosses the boundary

Soft targets logit distillation

The teacher's full probability distribution over the next token — "this token 70%, that one 20%". Richer than a single right answer, because the tail of the distribution carries the teacher's uncertainty.

Requires the student to have the same output vocabulary as the teacher, so that "token 4127" denotes the same event in both.

Also called: logit matching, response-based distillation
Hard targets sequence-level

Finished text. The teacher generates a summary; that summary is used as the training label. Nothing needs to be shared between the two models but the ability to read text.

Also called: sequence-level knowledge distillation, Seq-KD
Temperature soft targets only

Divides the teacher's logits before softmax, flattening the distribution so the small probabilities survive. Hinton's key trick. Irrelevant to you — it is a knob on a method you cannot use.

Listed so you recognise it when you meet it in a paper.
Vocabulary mismatch your blocker

Two models whose BPE merge tables were learned separately will split the same text into different tokens, so no index correspondence exists between them. This is not a matter of logit distillation being harder — it is undefined. There is no loss function to write.

Measured: teacher 151,669 · student 248,320. Verify with the exercise in §04 of Lesson 01.
Pseudo-labels the product

Labels that machine generated rather than a human wrote. In Seq-KD they are the teacher's summaries, and they replace the reference summaries as training targets — not supplement them.

Also called: synthetic data, silver labels, distillation targets

03How the teacher generates them

Beam search teacher-side

Decoding that keeps the k highest-scoring partial sequences alive at each step and returns the best complete one. Kim & Rush use it teacher-side because high-probability sequences make cleaner teaching targets.

Contrast: your existing results.jsonl used sampling at temperature 0.7 — tuned for deliverable quality, not target quality.
Greedy decoding student-side, measured

Always take the single most likely token. Kim & Rush found a Seq-KD student needs nothing better at inference — which matters enormously on a CPU, where beam search multiplies compute by the beam width. “Free” in that claim means free of beam-search compute, not free of consequences, and this project settled the difference by running it (2026-10-13). Serving the shipped Q4_K_M artifact greedily — same weights, engine and seed, decoding the only variable — cut ungrounded names from 37 on 26 calls to 24 on 16 calls and ungrounded numbers from 105 to 76, at an identical output length (108.7 words against 108.0). ROUGE did not improve in any way the run could detect: ROUGE-2 moved +0.0119 [+0.0035, +0.0193] while ROUGE-1 and ROUGE-L both tied, so the defensible sentence is not worse, not better. The costs are real on both sides: one of 144 calls hit the 900-token cap and ended mid-sentence where the sampled run had none (greedy cannot escape a loop it enters — the degeneration Holtzman et al. describe), copy rate rose slightly (0.0158 against 0.0117), and it takes 1.16× the wall time (median 18.5 s against 16.0 s). What it buys is determinism. It is also a net reduction rather than a per-call one: sampling invents more names on 19 calls, greedy on 8, 117 tie.

See lesson 06 and finetune/scores_greedy.json. Contrast: results.jsonl used sampling at temperature 0.7, which was never a measured choice until this run made it one.
On-policy vs off-policy if you go further

Off-policy: the student trains on the teacher's outputs for the training inputs (what Seq-KD does). On-policy: the student generates its own outputs and the teacher scores them, so training and inference see the same distribution.

In TRL's GKDTrainer, lmbda interpolates: 0 is off-policy SFT, 1 is fully on-policy.

On-Policy Distillation of Language Models (GKD)

04Failure modes

Abstractiveness the trap

How much a summary rephrases rather than copies. High abstractiveness means the model is genuinely summarizing; low means it is selecting sentences from the transcript.

DisCal found naive Seq-KD reduces abstractiveness.
Copy rate your existing metric

The share of summary n-grams that appear verbatim in the transcript. It is the operational proxy for (inverse) abstractiveness, and your eval/ scorer already reports it — which is what makes the DisCal trap testable for you rather than theoretical.

DisCal, 2023
Forward vs reverse KL capacity mismatch

Which direction the divergence is measured. Standard distillation minimizes forward KL, which forces the student to cover everything the teacher does — bad when the student is much smaller. Reverse KL lets the student be confidently narrow instead, and MiniLLM found it works better under large capacity gaps.

MiniLLM, 2023 · applies to soft-target distillation only.

05Project terms

House style what you are preserving

The project's summary conventions: 500–1200 characters, no SPEAKER_00 tags, a named speaker where identifiable, and closing lines like Next steps / Outcome signal. The distillation is only interesting if the 2B keeps this.

Deliverable set vs training set the coverage gap

Deliverable: the 144 calls the project was built to summarize — these are what results.jsonl holds teacher output for. Training: the 291 examples in train.jsonl + val.jsonl. The teacher has answered roughly 122 of the 291, so a teacher inference pass is required work, not a formality.

GGUF / quantization deployment

The file format and weight-compression used by llama.cpp, the class of runtime that runs models on CPU without PyTorch. The student ships as a 1.27 GB Q4_K_M file against 3.78 GB for the same weights in f16 — a factor of 2.96. About a quarter of either file is the tied embedding, because the vocabulary is 248,320.

It costs a measurable amount, and the amount is now known rather than assumed. Scored on the same 144 calls, references and scorer as every other number here, the deployed file takes 0.4285 ROUGE-1 against its own 16-bit self's 0.4447, served through the same engine at the same seed — 96% of itself, 89% of its teacher (0.4784). The gap is +0.0162 [+0.0057, +0.0266] on a paired bootstrap: small, and real. The engine and the draw, measured the same way, are worth nothing (−0.0048, a tie).

What it buys is speed, and only speed: f16 costs 1.85× the wall time per call (a median 29.0 s against 15.7 s), of which prefill is 1.54× and decode 2.51×. An earlier version of this entry also claimed robustness — that a memory-mapped 3.78 GB file could be paged out under pressure and collapse to 0.1 tok/s where the 1.27 GB file stays resident. Withdrawn 2026-10-13: the outlier behind it was a 1,936-second Windows Modern Standby suspend, entered after decoding had already begun, and no page was evicted. Size buys speed; it does not buy reliability.

Supported, and no longer an open risk: llama.cpp merged native qwen35 support in February 2026, and a fused CPU GATED_DELTA_NET op in March 2026, worth +34–41% generation on a Raspberry Pi 5. There is no official Qwen GGUF yet; bartowski and Unsloth are the publishers, and Unsloth also ships MTP speculative-decoding builds.
Prefill vs decode where the CPU time goes

Prefill is the model reading your transcript; decode is it writing the summary. On a CPU these are much closer in speed than on a GPU — measured here at roughly 6× rather than 20× — and a transcript is far longer than a summary. So prefill, not decode, is the larger share of the wall clock.

Measured on the deploy target — an Intel Core Ultra 9 285H, 16 threads, 100% CPU with no iGPU offload — over all 144 deliverable calls, the deployed student (Qwen3.5-2B, Q4_K_M) costs a median 16.0 s per call with a median 67% of it prefill: p90 26.6 s, worst case 33.0 s. Token-weighted throughput was 163 tok/s prefill and 27.7 tok/s decode, on a median prompt of 1,680 tokens. The second instrument, llama-bench on the same GGUF, lands in the same region but not on the same point — pp1764 206 tok/s against the runtime's 163, tg128 21.8 against 27.7 — so the two agree to within about a third. Close enough that neither is measuring a different machine; far enough that a throughput figure should never be quoted without naming the instrument that produced it.

An earlier revision of this entry gave ~74 s per call at ~70% prefill, taken by analogy from an RK3588 board. It was right about the shape — prefill is still the larger half — and about 5× wrong about the magnitude, and it was never a measurement of the machine the model ships on.

Consequence: prompt length is a first-class lever, not a rounding error — and caching only helps an exact repeat. Prefill scales with the full prompt length rather than with a shared preamble, so every new call pays its own. That is also why Qwen3.5-2B's chunked linear attention is an advantage here: most of its layers keep a constant-size state instead of a growing KV cache.

06Reading a training run

Training loss the number that can lie

The mean cross-entropy over the tokens the model was shown, at the run position you are reading. It is the objective the optimizer is minimising, which is exactly why it is not evidence of usefulness: the model is allowed to memorise, and memorising reduces it perfectly. In this project's arm run it was logged as the mean of the last 10 steps' micro-batches, with the window reset at every print — so a logged point is a window mean and, except in the last epoch, never covers a whole epoch.

Consequence: it can always be driven down, and a lower one is not a better model. At epoch 3 the control arm's training loss was 0.270 against the blend's 0.606 — and the control is the arm you must not ship.
Validation loss the number you hope predicts usefulness

The same cross-entropy, measured on rows held out of training — 12 calls here, with train/val call overlap measured at 0. It is the only one of the two numbers that is even attempting to answer "will this be any good on call 292?", and it moves independently of training loss, which is what makes a run readable at all.

Consequence: it is the one allowed to change your mind. On this run it rose in both arms — blend 1.579 → 1.709, control 1.633 → 2.326 — so it never licensed shipping either arm as trained; what it separated was how fast each was going wrong.
Generalization gap the readable quantity

Validation loss minus training loss, compared for the same run. It is more portable between two arms than either loss alone, because the shared difficulty of the same 12 held-out rows cancels out and what is left is how much the model has traded away to fit what it saw. It also makes a single seed less fatal, since both arms are reading identical validation rows.

Consequence: this is the quantity that made the arm comparison readable without error bars. At epoch 3 the gap was +1.10 for the blend against +2.06 for the control — the control was nearly twice as far from the held-out rows, on the same 12 rows, in the same step count.
Overfitting train down, val up

Training loss falling while validation loss rises: the model buying performance on the rows it was shown at the price of performance on rows it was not. It is a thing that happens over steps, not a property that small datasets have — the first epoch of this run shows neither arm overfitting, the control's gap still slightly negative.

Consequence: the schedule is the alibi to rule out first. This run's learning rate decayed monotonically (2e-4 cosine to 0), so anything driven by step size should have been settling; validation loss climbing under a decaying schedule is the model moving toward the training calls and away from the others.

07Judging two systems

Paired bootstrap the test that answers "is it better?"

Resample the per-call differences between two systems with replacement, recompute their mean, and repeat — 10,000 times here. The middle 95% of those resampled means is the interval. It is paired because the difference is taken call by call: the call's difficulty appears in both terms of the subtraction and cancels out. Pooling both systems' scores and resampling that instead throws the pairing away and returns a wider interval for identical data.

Consequence: this is the only instrument on this page that can say a difference is real. Blend − control over the same 144 calls is +0.0223 [+0.0087, +0.0365]; recomputing the four comparisons from the per-call rows reproduces the harness's own two published intervals to within 0.0007.
Confidence interval an interval, not a point

The 2.5th-to-97.5th percentile of the resampled means — where the mean difference plausibly lies given these calls. It is a statement about the mean, never about any individual call, and it does not bound the individual differences: they are far more spread out than the interval on their average.

Consequence: the whole reading rule is one line — if the interval contains zero, the difference is not distinguishable from noise at this sample size. That is not evidence of equality, only that 144 calls cannot separate them. Two of the four comparisons here cross zero (qwen4b − blend +0.0011 [−0.0112, +0.0134]; blend − honest +0.0027 [−0.0100, +0.0160]).
Win/loss count the number that lies

How many of the paired calls each system scored higher on. Cheap, intuitive, and not a test: it reports the direction of the majority and nothing about its size, so a system can win most calls and still be level overall.

Consequence: on these 144 calls Qwen3-4B beats the student on 81 of them and the paired test calls it a tie — because those wins average +0.0521 against losses averaging −0.0645. The count carries information only when the count and the average margin agree on the direction, as they do for blend − control (84/60, wins larger) and blend − teacher (42/102, losses larger).
Tail the part a mean hides

The bottom of a system's per-call distribution: the calls it handles badly. A mean cannot distinguish a system that is uniformly mediocre from one that is usually good and occasionally broken, and the two are very different things to ship.

Consequence: the student is more than 0.10 behind its teacher on 29 of 144 calls, scores below 0.25 on 11 of them, and bottoms out at 0.047 (the teacher's floor is 0.062, its median 0.4628). Read those calls before shipping either number — a mean of 0.4399 does not say whether the bad ones are rare embarrassments or a systematic failure on one kind of call.
