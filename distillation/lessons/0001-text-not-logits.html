Lesson 01 · Distilling the summarizer

Text, not logits Your teacher's token probabilities are unreachable from a Qwen3.5-2B — and that one fact picks your entire method for you.

By the end of this lesson you will know why this distillation has to be sequence-level rather than logit-based, what the three-step recipe looks like as a result, and the first thing you must re-run before any student training can start.

01Two ways to copy a teacher

Every distillation setup has a teacher, a student, and a question: what actually gets copied across? There are two answers, and they are not variations on a theme — they need different machinery.

The soft-target route comes from Hinton, Vinyals & Dean (2015). You don't copy the teacher's final answer, you copy its uncertainty: at every position the student is trained to match the teacher's whole probability distribution over the next token — "this one 70%, that one 20%, that one 4%". It is a far richer signal than a single right answer, and it is why distillation can beat training on gold labels alone.

The hard-target route comes from Kim & Rush (2016). You don't copy probabilities at all. You run the teacher over your training inputs to produce finished outputs, and then you train the student on those outputs as if they were gold labels. Plain supervised fine-tuning. The teacher's knowledge arrives as text.

02Why the soft-target route is closed to you

Soft targets require one assumption: that when the teacher says "token 4127 has probability 0.7", the student has a token 4127 that means the same thing. Teacher and student must share an output space.

Yours don't. I read the tokenizer sitting in your qwen3-8b-lora/ directory:

Teacher
Qwen3-8B — 151,669 tokens
Student
Qwen3.5-2B — 248,320 tokens
Shared
nothing, structurally — different merge tables

The sizes are the symptom, not the disease. BPE vocabularies are learned, so the two models don't merely have different-length token lists — they split the same sentence into different pieces. "Kennedy" might be one token to one model and two to the other.

The precise reason

This is not a matter of logit distillation being harder across tokenizers. It is undefined. You would be asking the student to match a distribution over a set of events it does not have. There is no loss function to write.

03So the recipe picks itself

Sequence-level distillation is the only option — which is convenient, because Kim & Rush found it is also the one that works well for exactly this kind of task. Three steps, and you already have the tooling for the third:

  1. Feed the teacher your training prompts. The user messages in your training split — the same build_model_input() text the 8B was trained on, but deliberately not the same records. §05 explains why that distinction is the entire reason step 1 is expensive.
  2. Collect its summaries as pseudo-labels. The teacher's output becomes the target. No reference summaries involved.
  3. Train the student with plain SFT on {prompt → teacher summary} pairs. The same SFTTrainer you already know.

Decode with beam search, not sampling. Kim & Rush's "approximate" mode uses the teacher's beam search because it surfaces higher-probability sequences, giving the student cleaner targets. Your existing results.jsonl was generated with do_sample=True, temperature=0.7 — that was tuned for producing deliverable summaries, and it is not the same job as producing teaching targets.

A bonus you get for free

Kim & Rush also found Seq-KD students do not need beam search at inference — greedy decoding suffices. That is exactly what you want on a CPU, where beam search multiplies your compute by the beam width.

04Check the claim on your own machine

Exercise · 3 minutes

Measure the vocabulary gap yourself

Don't take the table in §02 on trust. Download both tokenizers (they are small — no model weights) and measure the overlap. The last number is the one that matters.

vocab_gap.py 
from transformers import AutoTokenizer

teacher = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained("Qwen/Qwen3-8B")     # the 8B you trained
student = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained("Qwen/Qwen3.5-2B")   # the 2B you want

tv, sv = teacher.get_vocab(), student.get_vocab()
print("teacher vocab            :", len(tv))
print("student vocab            :", len(sv))

shared = set(tv) & set(sv)
print("shared token strings     :", len(shared))

# The number that decides the method: how many shared strings also sit at
# the SAME index in both vocabularies. Logit distillation needs this to be
# large. Everything else is decoration.
same_index = sum(1 for k in shared if tv[k] == sv[k])
print("shared at the same index :", same_index)

# And the tokenizers disagree about how to split text at all:
for word in ("Kennedy", "acquisitions", " $2.4M"):
    print(repr(word), "->", teacher.tokenize(word), "|", student.tokenize(word))

What confirms the lesson: the two vocab sizes differ, and "shared at the same index" is tiny relative to either — far too small to support per-token probability matching. The last loop will usually show the two tokenizers cutting the same word into different pieces. Your exact counts will shift slightly with your transformers version; the shape of the result is what you are reading.

05Two things to know before you start

Your teacher has answered none of the questions

results.jsonl holds 144 teacher summaries. They were generated over the deliverable calls — the 144 your project exists to summarize — and there is a deliberate reason your student must not train on any of them.

Of the 291 training records, 137 are deliverable calls. Training the student on those would teach it the answers to the very benchmark you are about to grade it on. The student would look better than it is, and its score would not be comparable to the 4B and 8B numbers you already have — both of which were trained on a clean split.

So the student trains on the 154 records that are not deliverable calls — 142 train, 12 validation. That split already exists on disk, as call-summarizer/finetune/train.jsonl. And the teacher's 144 summaries cover zero of those 154: the two sets are disjoint by construction.

Which is why step 1 of the recipe is the whole job rather than a formality. Every pseudo-label has to be generated from scratch. make_infer_input.py already knows how to rebuild these prompts from dataset_raw.json; it points at the delivered calls today, and needs pointing at the training ones instead.

The trap that costs people a week

DisCal (2023) found that naive sequence-level distillation reduces abstractiveness — it makes the student copy more from the transcript rather than summarizing it. Seq-KD targets are fluent and confident, and a small student imitates the fluency while losing the abstraction.

This is testable for you rather than theoretical: your eval/ scorer already reports copy rate. If the student's copy rate climbs while ROUGE holds steady, this is what happened — and the fix is generating several candidate summaries per call and keeping the ones that score well on both informativeness and novelty, not just taking the top beam.
