Lesson 04 · Distillation

Evaluate honestly The student ties the 4B. It loses to its own teacher — by a margin that is small, real, and reportable.

Lesson 03 read the loss curves. This lesson reads the scores. The first thing to notice is that a table of six mean ROUGE-1 values cannot answer a single one of the questions you actually have about them.

01 Six variants, one scorer, 144 calls

Everything below comes from one scored run: 144 deliverable calls, each summarised by six systems and each scored against the same human-written reference summary, by one scorer, in one pass. That shared frame is what makes the columns comparable at all — a ROUGE-1 value means nothing except relative to the reference it was measured against.

Mean reference length is 151.9 words, so every Qwen-family variant is in the right length neighbourhood, and BART is not: it copies sentences out of the transcript, which is why its mid-sentence-end count is 47 and its copy rate is 20× everyone else's. BART is in the table as a floor, not as a competitor.

Read the table the way it is usually read and you get an ordering and nothing else: teacher 0.4784, then 4B 0.4410, blend 0.4399, untuned-8B 0.4372, control 0.4176, BART 0.2007. Is the blend's 0.4399 against the 4B's 0.4410 a loss? A win? Nothing in the table says, and there is no way to find out by staring harder. Those six numbers are means over 144 calls, and the calls are not interchangeable.

02 Pair the calls, or measure nothing

Some of these 144 calls are easy: two speakers, a clear ask, a decision at the end. Others are four speakers, three topics, and a transcript that starts mid-sentence. A variant's mean ROUGE is therefore partly a statement about which calls it saw. Here every variant saw all 144, so the call population is held fixed — but that does not help a naive comparison, because the call's difficulty still sits inside each mean, adding variance to both.

The fix is to compare per call. Take the 144 differences rouge1(blend) − rouge1(control), one per call. Now the call's difficulty is in both terms of the subtraction, so it cancels: an easy call contributes a small difference, a hard call contributes a small difference, and what remains is the systematic part. Then resample the 144 calls with replacement, ten thousand times, recomputing the mean difference each time, and read the interval off the middle 95% of those resamples. This is the paired bootstrap.

The rule that makes it useful: if the interval contains zero, the difference is not distinguishable from noise at this sample size. That is not a claim that the two systems are equal — only that 144 calls cannot tell them apart.

Run it yourself

Recompute the one comparison everything else hangs on

You do not have to take my intervals on trust. The bootstrap is twenty lines of standard library, and the per-call scores are already sitting in the harness output. From finetune/: 

import json, random, statistics

rows = json.load(open("student_scores.json", encoding="utf-8"))["rows"]
blend   = {r["call_id"]: r["scores"]["rouge1"] for r in rows["student_blend"]}
control = {r["call_id"]: r["scores"]["rouge1"] for r in rows["student_control"]}

delta = [blend[c] - control[c] for c in sorted(blend)]        # paired, per call
rng = random.Random(0)
means = sorted(statistics.fmean(rng.choices(delta, k=len(delta))) for _ in range(10_000))

lo, hi = means[250], means[9749]                              # 2.5th and 97.5th
wins = sum(d > 0 for d in delta)
print(f"mean {statistics.fmean(delta):+.4f}  CI [{lo:+.4f}, {hi:+.4f}]  W{wins}/L{len(delta) - wins}")

You should land on mean +0.0223 CI [+0.0087, +0.0365] W84/L60: the student is ahead of the control by about two ROUGE-1 points, and the interval does not contain zero. Swap the two dicts' order and every sign flips, which is the sanity check that you have actually paired the calls rather than pooled them — pooling throws the pairing away and gives you a wider interval for the same data.

03 Four comparisons, four shapes

That is one comparison. The four worth having are below, each a paired bootstrap over the same 144 calls. W/L is the raw per-call win and loss count — and it is the column that will mislead you if you let it.

blend − teacher−0.0385[−0.0518, −0.0258]42 / 102real deficit

The row that will trip you up is the second one. The 4B beats the student on 81 of 144 calls — a clear majority — and the paired test calls it a tie. Both facts are correct. The student's 63 wins average +0.0521 while its 63 losses average −0.0645: it wins more often and loses bigger, and the two cancel to +0.0011 with an interval straddling zero. A win count is not a mean, and 81/63 is not a result. Only when both the count and the average margin point the same way — as in rows 1 and 4, where the blend's wins are larger than its losses and the teacher's losses are larger than its wins — does the count tell you anything.

Row three is the flattest result on the page: 71–72 against the untuned 8B, on a mean of +0.0027. Distillation bought the small student nothing measurable against its own base model at 8B scale, and the honest reading is that the untuned 8B was already summarising this corpus about as well as a 2B model can be taught to.

04 Where the student is worse

The mission's bar is "almost as good as the teacher". The measured answer is 0.4399 against 0.4784 — about 92% of the teacher's ROUGE-1, against a reference set neither model saw in training. That is the good news, and it is real: the deployed model is genuinely close.

The rest of the answer is that the gap is not noise. The interval [−0.0518, −0.0258] lies entirely below zero, and the teacher wins outright on 102 of 144 calls. On 29 calls the student is more than 0.10 behind. Its own distribution has a bad tail: 11 of the 144 calls score below 0.25 ROUGE-1, with a minimum of 0.047, while the teacher's floor is 0.062 and its median is 0.4628. So the honest sentence is:

The student is a usable model that is measurably worse than its teacher, by a small but real margin, with a thin tail of calls it handles badly.

That sentence is worth more than the number in it, because it is the sentence a deployment decision can be made from. "92% of the teacher" invites you to ship without looking; "measurably worse, with a bad tail on 11 calls" tells you to go and read those 11 calls before you do.

05 Two aggregates that lie

Two more numbers from the table above that mean less than they appear to, both in the same way: an aggregate over 144 calls is not a description of 144 calls.

The control's 512 ungrounded names

512 versus the blend's 46 reads as an 11× failure of factuality, and it is one call. Call 116308713889 degenerates into a Roy/Royalty repetition loop: 456 words, duplicate rate 0.967, no sentence ending. It alone contributes 443 of the 512. Drop it and the control has 69 ungrounded names across 44 calls, against the blend's 46 across 30 — about 1.5×, on a smaller output length. The real finding is modest (the control fabricates somewhat more), and the spectacular one was a single broken decode wearing 144 calls' worth of authority.

The length story that isn't there

Reading a handful of bad outputs suggested the student fails by being too long or too short. It does not. Output length correlates with reference length at +0.091 for the blend — and +0.081 for the teacher, +0.090 for the 4B, +0.014 for the control. Nobody, including the model we are treating as the target, tracks the reference's length. Mean absolute length error is 66.5 words for the blend against 66.4 for the teacher. Length explains r = 0.016 of the teacher–blend gap, i.e. nothing. If you had gone fixing length, you would have spent a week changing a number uncorrelated with the metric you were trying to move.

Ask me

Both of these were caught by decomposing an aggregate by call, and you can point me at any suspicious number in the table and ask for the same treatment. "Which calls produce the blend's 46 ungrounded names?" is the obvious next question; so is "what are the 11 calls under 0.25?"

06 What this cannot claim

Four limits, stated because the lesson is about evaluating honestly and a lesson that only lists its wins is not doing that.

  • There is no untuned-2B arm. The comparison that exists is blend versus control: the same 284 training rows, the same steps, differing only in whether the second half of the file is teacher summaries or the same references again. That licenses "teacher supervision beat more reference supervision at matched volume". It does not license "distillation beat the base model" — that arm was never run.
  • The blend confounds supervision with variety. The control's 284 rows come from 142 calls seen twice; the blend's come from 142 references plus 142 teacher summaries of the same calls. Volume, steps and seed are matched; the diversity of what the model saw is not. Part of the +0.0223 may be variety rather than teacher quality, and nothing in this run separates the two. This is D6, and it is an unexcluded rival explanation, not a footnote.
  • The scorer is not in this repository. It lives in the sibling call-summarizer/eval/ project, so the ordering above is reproducible here but the absolute values are not — an independent unigram-overlap F1 reproduction gives the same ordering about 0.03 lower. Treat the differences as the findings and the levels as the scorer's dialect.
  • The model you would actually ship is still unmeasured. Every number here is the LoRA adapter evaluated in 16-bit. The deployed artifact is the same weights at Q4_K_M in a GGUF, and the instrument built to check the quantizer returns agreement with a 16-bit reference at the prefix level — which any paraphrase fails. The four-bit model's quality on these 144 calls has not been scored. That is the open item, not a closed question.
